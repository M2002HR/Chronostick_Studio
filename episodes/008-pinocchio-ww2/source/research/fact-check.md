# Historical review — Pinocchio and World War II

Research date: 2026-10-05. Supplied video is creative reference, not historical authority. Draft adaptation; no creative approval.

| Source claim | Evidence and decision |
| --- | --- |
| Pinocchio opened in 1940 during World War II | Supported. D23 dates the New York premiere to February 7, 1940. Narration uses only the year, avoiding confusion between premiere and wider release. |
| The film cost $2.6 million | Supported by D23. Keep the spoken amount; no generated numerals or money labels. |
| The war hindered foreign distribution and the initial release failed financially | Supported by the Walt Disney Family Museum and Disney's official history. This is the main causal explanation retained. |
| Families could not afford tickets; this massively reduced revenue | Source assertion; not separately established by the reviewed sources as the main cause. Drop the poor-family gag and avoid treating all cinemas as closed. A sparsely occupied auditorium is a symbolic illustration of weak receipts. |
| First-run revenue was exactly $1.4 million | Omit. Historical accounting distinguishes rentals, gross receipts and studio recovery. Reviewed primary institutional pages establish failure without requiring this exact comparison. |
| Pinocchio and Mickey appeared in the displayed military poster | The source poster is an observed source illustration, not authenticated archival evidence. Do not recreate it or assert the feature itself was propaganda. |
| Propaganda restored Pinocchio's profits | Unsupported causal link. The museum says military training production helped the studio survive, and quotes Walt's at-cost policy. Keep studio survival distinct from the feature's receipts. |
| By 1945 the feature had earned $84 million in profits | Reject from the adaptation. The Numbers reports approximately $84.3 million as cumulative domestic box office after the 1992 rerelease, not 1945 profits. D23 verifies a 1945 rerelease without that earnings claim. |
| Disney became rich from the campaign | Drop the cash-throne ending and marketing question. Replace with the contrast between an initially unsuccessful release and an enduring classic. |

Sources, directly consulted:

1. [D23 — Pinocchio (film)](https://d23.com/a-to-z/pinocchio-film/): premiere year/date, production cost, rereleases including 1945, awards and historical status.
2. [Walt Disney Family Museum — Paving the Way for Success: The Importance of Pinocchio](https://www.waltdisney.org/blog/paving-way-success-importance-pinocchio): initial box-office failure, loss of foreign markets, later cultural importance, wartime at-cost work.
3. [D23 — Disney History](https://d23.com/disney-history/): expensive 1940 features, wartime loss of foreign markets, military training/propaganda production and difficult postwar recovery.
4. [Walt Disney Family Museum — To Honor Those Who Serve](https://www.waltdisney.org/blog/honor-those-who-serve): military training production helped prevent studio bankruptcy; at-cost policy. Do not convert this to film profit.
5. [The Numbers — Pinocchio (1940)](https://www.the-numbers.com/movie/Pinocchio-(1940)): July 3, 1992 cumulative domestic box-office figure of $84.3 million. This supports the accounting/time-period correction, not a claim about 1945 earnings.

Visual integrity: no D-Day-type landing before the February 1940 premiere; no literal Pinocchio aboard a torpedoed ship; no wartime victims as jokes. Studio work appears after the initial release, without implying it was all commissioned in 1940. The 1945 return is shown without assuming every conflict had already ended on its rerelease date.
