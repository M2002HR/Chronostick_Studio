## Visual and continuity grammar

The thumbnail-size identity lock for Napoleon is a short, compact ChronoStick silhouette; black bicorne, dark blue early-nineteenth-century coat, pale waistcoat, white breeches, black boots, one hand frequently at the coat. His face follows the off-white round-head style with tiny black eyes. The hat and coat—not photorealistic facial imitation—carry recognition. The approved master style is the rendering authority. The existing late-eighteenth-century France world and guard sheet are useful design history, but their period mismatch means neither becomes a direct 1814 H3 reference.

Color tells the story: cold slate and rust for defeat; candle gold and parchment for the bargain; sea teal and three-bee red/white/gold for Elba; red dawn and navy for escape; muted French tricolor returning only after landing. The seven-islands-in-London visual is an unlabeled symbolic scale comparison, not an 1814 London scene. Shapes and landmarks must stay legible without on-screen text.

Every five-second clip has its own resolved final image. Graphic matches may link completed shots (crown to island, wax seal to sun, flag stripe to sail, hoof to drum, ship prow to shore), but no generated clip needs a pixel-matched continuation. Background crowds are simple silhouettes. Even with rapid cuts, Napoleon never duplicates in a frame, and the Elba flag, brig, and uniform details stay consistent.
