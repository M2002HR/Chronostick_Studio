## What the reference teaches about pace and humor

- The piece alternates long stable character scenes, quick graphic bridges, maps and battle cuts. Its energetic feel is built from *changes in narrative mode*, with pauses for the audience to understand geography and succession. A long-form ChronoStick direction should test this variation rather than impose one cut density throughout.
- Humor is strongest where a person's expectation collides with the documented outcome (the opening, an official's confidence, a reversal). Keep joke targets to power, miscalculation or irony; do not use the deaths or enslaved people as a punchline.
- The visual system repeatedly reuses locations, costumes and map scales. Our H3 workflow needs distinct approved scene anchors and continuity states to reproduce that clarity across independent five-second generations.
- Soundtrack and spoken character dialogue in the reference are examples of its presentation, not audio instructions for ChronoStick. The project requires separate Spanish narration, natural isolated SFX, and no background music or generated dialogue.

## First historical risk triage (not Stage 01 approval)

1. **War duration and clock:** The [Historical Association](https://www.history.org.uk/publications/categories/301/resource/7950/the-shortest-war-in-history-the-anglo-zanzibar-wa) notes published durations of 38, 40 and 43 minutes; [Guinness World Records](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/65353-shortest-war) gives 9:00–9:45. Keep the central *shortest recorded war* claim, but source the exact start/end used for any spoken or on-screen clock.
2. **Colonial status:** [The UK National Archives](https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/migrated-archives-8-tranche-guide.pdf) identifies Zanzibar as a British protectorate in this period. The video's dialogue that Britain simply “owned” the island is a dramatized simplification; maps and narration need accurate status language.
3. **Slavery chronology:** [The UK National Archives](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/education/resources/slavery/source-2a/) dates Zanzibar's abolition to **1897**. The transcript's immediate postwar abolition claim must not be repeated without correction. Britain's 1807 trade act and 1833 abolition act also covered different practices and territories; see the [National Archives overview](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/education/resources/slavery/).
4. **Deaths and injuries:** The [Historical Association](https://www.history.org.uk/publications/categories/301/resource/7950/the-shortest-war-in-history-the-anglo-zanzibar-wa) and [South African History Online](https://sahistory.org.za/article/anglo-zanzibar-war-1896) describe the toll differently. Separate dead from wounded and do not turn the single British injury into an unsupported slapstick cause.
5. **Names, poisoning and exile:** The transcript visibly garbles Harry Rawson, Hamad bin Thuwaini, Hamoud bin Mohammed and Khalid bin Barghash. Treat the poison allegation, exact kinship, attributed dialogue and later exile route as research questions. The [Historical Association](https://www.history.org.uk/publications/categories/301/resource/7950/the-shortest-war-in-history-the-anglo-zanzibar-wa) and a [scholarly study of Khalid](https://revistas.ucm.es/index.php/ANQE/en/article/view/ANQE1010110035A) are starting points, not a completed claim ledger.
